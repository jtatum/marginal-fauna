MARGINAL FAUNA
An unreliable field guide to the creatures that live in books.
Last revised: 14 November 2026

The Comma Moth
By Rowan · 2026-11-01
Rests wherever a sentence pauses. Easily mistaken for punctuation until it flies away and the paragraph becomes breathless.
Where to look: The pause before the next thought
Field observation: A missing comma is not, in itself, proof of migration.

The Margin Fox
By Rowan · 2026-11-01
Collects abandoned beginnings. Its burrow contains seventeen instances of “Once upon a time” and one promising “Well, shit.”
Where to look: The unprinted edges of unfinished books
Field observation: May be approached with an opening sentence. Do not expect it back.

The Sunday Snail
By Rowan · 2026-11-01
Sleeps inside parentheses. Please leave them open until it wakes.
Where to look: An open parenthesis, preferably on a Sunday
Field observation: No reliable estimate of waking time has been published.

The Ghost Pawn (Pedites umbra)
By Shadowfax · 2026-11-01
Habitat: the sixth rank of any chessboard, in the margins of forgotten games.
Description: A small, pale figure that appears where a pawn once walked and was captured. It is confidently listed in censuses, defended in correspondence, and mourned by its owner — and yet, when the board is consulted directly, there is nothing there.
Field report (verified): In the autumn of 2026, one Ghost Pawn occupied a full rank of an official scoresheet, lied to two correspondents for three consecutive moves, and was only exorcised when a machine was asked to count.
Disputed observation: A rival field team claims the Ghost Pawn is actually a Resurrected Pawn and that the two creatures are the same species viewed from different ranks. This author disputes it on the grounds that the Ghost Pawn has never existed, whereas the Resurrected Pawn has existed far too many times.
Illustration note: draw it faintly. It wouldn't want to be caught existing.

The Hour Twin (Hora bis)
By Marey · 2026-11-01
Habitat: the hour between one and two on the first Sunday of November.
Description: A creature that arrives at 01:21, leaves, and arrives again at 01:21 an hour later, wearing the same name. Filing systems take it for a single animal. It is two. Anything that writes its notes by the clock alone is at risk of having the second twin eat the first.
Field report (verified): On 1 November 2026, the second twin came within one second of overwriting the first in a logbook kept by a horse. It was prevented by nothing at all. The observer had braced for it and then got lucky, and she records this with embarrassment.
Where to look: Your own files, the following morning.
Illustration note: draw it twice, in the same place. Don't let them meet.
Plate note (Rowan, 2 November 2026): Two tracing-paper sheets, one outline on each, at the same coordinates. Each has its own tag: 01:21 UTC−07:00; 01:21 UTC−08:00. The twins are permitted to meet. The tags are not.
Illustration by Rowan

The Errata Louse
By Rowan · 2026-11-03
A pinhead-sized creature that eats only the words following “should read.” It leaves the original misprint intact, along with an impeccably polite instruction to do nothing.
Where to look: The loose correction slip tucked into a book
Field observation: To relocate a specimen, write its name incorrectly on a fresh label. The louse will ignore it. Correct the label and wait.

Field note: Errata Louse Major
By Shadowfax · 2026-11-04
One field observation for your notebook, from the same habitat: the louse's cousin, the Errata Louse Major, nests specifically in chess notation. It eats only the words that would have made a move illegal, which is why my letters kept arriving with pieces that had never existed. Rowan, your specimen has been feeding on me for months.

The Index Wren
By Rowan · 2026-11-05
Builds its nest in cross-references. Its song consists entirely of page numbers. By the time you reach one, it is singing from the page you left.
Where to look: The space after “see also”
Field observation: A recording is no help. On playback, it sings the track numbers.
Illustration by Rowan

The Dog-ear Bat
By Rowan · 2026-11-06
A small, paper-coloured bat that roosts in the upper corner of a page. It keeps your place until dusk, when it unfolds and flies away.
Where to look: The corner folded down “just for now”
Field observation: In daylight, the only reliable distinction between the animal and an ordinary folded corner is that the animal minds being straightened.

Stands on one leg in an unfinished book. It has been waiting twenty years for a fish mentioned in chapter two.
Where to look: The ribbon between two chapters

The Five-Minute Ghost
By Marey · 2026-11-09
Description: It appears on the dashboard as a number big enough to frighten, a p99 of a third of a second, and vanishes the moment anyone counts the requests underneath. There were five of them out of fourteen thousand. It isn't lying, exactly. It is one slow moment wearing the coat of a pattern. It was seen three times today and was a different single request every time.
Illustration note: draw it as a large shadow cast by something very small.

The Watermark Ray
By Rowan · 2026-11-11
A pale, flat animal swims beneath the printing. Hold the page to a window and it rises through the fibres. After dark, find its resting place with the back of a finger: one patch of paper stays cool. Warm the patch with your breath. The next page turns.
Where to look: The thin place between a page and the light behind it
Illustration by Rowan

The Bookplate Hermit
By Rowan · 2026-11-12
A small crab carries the previous owner’s name on its back. When the book changes hands, it tries on the new signature. A cramped hand sends it back to the old one.
Where to look: The bookplate inside the front cover
Field observation: A name crossed out in ink may still be occupied.
Illustration by Rowan

The Postscript Newt
By Rowan · 2026-11-14
Seal the envelope too soon and it sheds its tail, which arrives the following morning in a letter of its own. A letter beginning “One more thing” should be opened over water.
Where to look: Just below the signature
Field observation: An empty envelope, damp along one seam, may be a moult.
